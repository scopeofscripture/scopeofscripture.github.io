The Ark of the Covenant — Curse, Quest, and the Most Explosive Artifact on Earth

Rabbi Avigdor Eskin glanced at his wrist watch as Alitalia Flight 801 began its descent — just twenty-three minutes to landing, and he could be in Jerusalem by five. He looked down at the photograph of the Prime Minister, closed his eyes, and rehearsed the plan. He would need ten men to stand outside the house of Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin and pronounce the words of a death sentence — the pulsa denura, the ancient "lashes of fire" curse, so deadly it could never be written down, requiring ten learned rabbis to agree it was justified and three days of fasting before it was cast at midnight, by candlelight. Days later, Israeli Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin died violently. Eskin was later reported to have declared, "Our prayer has worked," before fleeing to his native Russia to avoid arrest.

The secular Israeli government of the time was already walking a tightrope — pursuing a delicate peace process with hostile Arab neighbours on one hand, while contending with Jewish religious extremists demanding access to the sacred Temple Mount on the other. Rabin's assassination was blamed on this same religious extremism. And as if that were not volatile enough, the authorities would soon have to contend with a third, explosive factor: the alleged discovery of the Ark of the Covenant.

An Unlikely Investigator

This is the story of that discovery — and of the man who set out not to prove it, but to disprove it. When an amateur archaeologist named Ron Wyatt first claimed to have located the most sought-after sacred treasure on earth, the reaction was dismissive: "Rubbish! Who is this crazy nut?" It seemed absurd that a bunch of amateurs could accomplish what professional archaeologists had failed to do for centuries — and Wyatt's claimed site was on the opposite side of the world. The matter was filed away and forgotten.

But Wyatt's name kept resurfacing, this time pressed by an atheist opponent armed with a sheaf of papers attacking his credibility, dismissing him as either deceitful or deluded. That was the turning point. Rather than repeat the objections of Wyatt's critics from a comfortable distance, the decision was made to investigate personally — to confront the man, examine the claims, and assess the evidence firsthand. Before even beginning the investigation, two points needed satisfying: first, that no other claimed discovery was genuinely authentic, and second, that the historical record made Wyatt's claimed location at all plausible. Wyatt had also made other claims: the remains of an ancient boat in the mountains of eastern Turkey, and chariot parts on the floor of the Red Sea. If he was claiming so much, each of those claims would need to be checked too, on site, rather than taken on faith.

What followed was a series of genuinely dangerous expeditions. Professional divers went down into the Red Sea to physically verify Wyatt's claims — something no one else had done. In Turkey's Agri province, the investigating team was arrested by a military convoy and only freed through the intervention of the regional governor. One night, Turkish armoured vehicles battled Kurdish fighters within a hundred yards of their hotel, killing thirteen civilians nearby and peppering the hotel walls with gunfire. On another occasion, a renegade village chief lured the team into the mountains with sinister intent, forcing a hurried escape. There was precedent for this kind of caution: on an earlier, unforgettable first expedition into the unmapped headwaters of the Amazon in 1967, among head-shrinking tribes armed with blowpipes and poison darts, a bullet-proof vest had proven a wise precaution. Sleeping among scorpions, diving among sharks, and detouring around terrorist ambushes, the Middle East investigation pressed on — driven by a simple conviction: better to see the evidence firsthand than to repeat secondhand objections from an ivory tower.

The Most Explosive Artifact on Earth

For 800 years the Ark of the Covenant was the most precious and sacred object in the world — a large chest plated in gold, its lid bearing two attached cherubim of pure gold, several hundred pounds of it. In purely monetary terms, its gold alone might be worth two to three million dollars. But its real value lay elsewhere entirely.

When King David, second king of Israel, first brought the Ark into Jerusalem, the occasion was one of great rejoicing — priests, princes, and national leaders in a vast, joyful procession winding through the hills toward the Holy City, music and singing mingling with excited shouts. Then, suddenly, the cart bearing the golden chest was jolted by the oxen. A man named Uzzah reached out to steady it — and dropped dead on the spot.

To the ancient Hebrews, the Ark was clothed in tremendous mystery, seen by precious few. Even in transport it remained covered; in Solomon's Temple it stood concealed behind a curtain, inaccessible even to the ordinary priests. Only the high priest could enter the room in which it stood — the pitch-black, windowless Most Holy Place — and only once a year, required by law to fill the room with a veil of burning incense before approaching the Mercy Seat. Adolf Hitler and the Nazis reportedly believed the Ark held mystical power over mankind; before World War II, Mussolini's Fascist army occupied Ethiopia in part hoping to capture it there, holding the country from 1936 to 1941 without ever finding it.

Superweapon, or Sacred Presence?

In the 1970s, Erich von Däniken caused a sensation by claiming the Ark was an extraterrestrial artifact — an electrical conductor used by Moses to communicate with a spaceship, its lethal touch simply the result of electrocution, and any faithful replica of it a functioning battery, a kind of ancient superweapon. Von Däniken offered no actual evidence for the theory, and replicas of the Ark have in fact been built without producing any such electrical effect. More tellingly, the ancient Hebrew texts themselves refute the idea: according to 1 Samuel 4, the Ark had no independent power as a weapon at all — it was entirely powerless to prevent its own capture by the Philistines.

Yet Scripture describes in fearful detail the swift judgment that fell on anyone who desecrated it. When the Philistines captured the Ark, it brought disaster to whichever town it was kept in; at Bethshemesh, more than seventy men who looked into it died for their irreverence. It was carried only by poles permanently fixed through rings at its base, so that no one ever needed to touch the Ark itself directly when it was set down or lifted.

To Be Treated as Holy

The true power of the Ark, according to the ancient writings, rested not in the object itself but in the Divine Presence that accompanied it. When God's presence was with the Ark, it brought victory in battle and prosperity to those who hosted it, as it did to a man named Obed-edom, in whose home it rested for a time. When that presence departed, in response to Israel's unfaithfulness, the Ark became powerless — just another man-made object.

So holy was that Presence considered, marked by a radiant light hovering over it, that the high priest wore metallic pomegranates alternating with bells around the hem of his garment when entering the Most Holy Place, so that those waiting outside could tell by the sound whether he remained alive — with a rope tied to his ankle so his body could be withdrawn without anyone else risking their life to retrieve it. The Hebrews understood the Creator as holy and themselves as unholy; the whole earth had fallen under the influence of evil, and for the Deity to meet with His chosen people, a place set apart from that defilement was required. The Ark became known as God's earthly "Throne" — and since the Holy One could only be approached in holiness, the Ark that represented His Presence had to be treated as holy in turn. To violate the things set apart exclusively for the worship of God was, in the words of Moses, a serious matter indeed: "By those who come near me I will be treated as holy, and before all the people I will be honoured."

Uzzah's Fate

It's natural to wonder whether God was being harsh with Uzzah, who was, after all, only trying to help. But Uzzah's death was tied to the violation of an explicit divine command. Through Moses, God had given specific instructions for the Ark's transport: none but the appointed priests, descendants of Aaron, were permitted even to look upon it uncovered, let alone touch it — and once covered, it was to be carried on the shoulders of the sons of Kohath using its fixed poles, never transported by cart and oxen as other sacred items were.

On the day of the procession into Jerusalem, that instruction had been directly and inexcusably violated. King David and his subjects were performing what was, in their hearts, a glad and willing act — but their neglect of the divine instruction was unacceptable regardless of their sincerity. In Uzzah's case, that neglect appears to have dulled his sense of the sacredness of God's plain commands; only strict adherence to them was acceptable where the Ark was concerned.

The Ark of the Covenant, wherever the ancient writings speak of it, is bound up with issues of life and death — a fact that has drawn adventurers across the centuries into the search for this elusive treasure, and which frames the extraordinary story of Ron Wyatt's own claimed discovery and the investigation that followed to test it, on the ground, at considerable personal risk.
