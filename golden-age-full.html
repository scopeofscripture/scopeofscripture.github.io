The Golden Age — Three Traditions, One Final Chapter

The term "the Golden Age" has become popularised recently by the President of the United States, Donald Trump. In his inaugural address, he declared that "the golden age of America begins right now." But the Golden Age has a much longer history than any single presidency. It relates to a period very early in the earth's history, as described by the ancients — and all over the world, the term revives memories of a utopian world of perfection. History demonstrates that the ancients held to a view of two Golden Ages: one before a great Flood, and one that developed after it.

Today, the term has taken on other applications — political, cultural, and, as this study focuses on, theological. Here we turn to three Abrahamic religious systems, each of which derives its origins from claims of descent from Abraham, and each of which has developed its own distinctive expectation of a coming Golden Age at the end of history.

Few questions have occupied the human imagination more persistently than the question of how history ends. Across cultures, centuries, and civilisations, the conviction that the present order of things is not final — that something decisive lies ahead, something that will resolve the tensions, injustices, and incompleteness of the present age — has proven remarkably resilient. It surfaces in mythology, in philosophy, and above all in the great monotheistic religious traditions that have shaped the moral and political landscape of the world for three millennia.

This study focuses on three traditions — Judaism, Christianity (specifically in its Dispensational expression), and Islam. For the Islamic position, this study draws particularly on the eschatological framework of the contemporary Islamic scholar Dr. Imran Hosein, who has done much to pioneer a systematic treatment of Islamic eschatology, a field that had little developed tradition of its own until his work over the last two decades. All three traditions are Abrahamic, tracing their origins to the covenant made between God and Abraham. All three regard Jerusalem as a city of unique sacred significance. And all three anticipate, in their own distinctive ways, a final chapter of human history in which divine sovereignty is fully and visibly established over a world that has, for most of its duration, resisted it.

Yet for all their shared geography and shared ancestry, these three traditions arrive at the end of history by very different roads, and disagree — often profoundly — about who the central figure of that final chapter will be, what he will do, how history will conclude, and what lies beyond it. This study examines those agreements and disagreements systematically and charitably, allowing each tradition to speak on its own terms before drawing comparative conclusions, and resisting the temptation to flatten genuine differences in the service of an artificial harmony.

Jerusalem: One Stage, Three Traditions

Jerusalem is the only city on earth that three major world religions simultaneously regard as the centre of history's final chapter. Judaism anticipates the arrival of a Davidic Messiah who will rebuild the Temple and inaugurate an era of universal peace centred there. Islam expects the return of the Prophet Isa (Jesus) to defeat the Antichrist and establish divine justice across the earth. Dispensational Christianity awaits the Second Coming of Jesus Christ, a seven-year tribulation, and a thousand-year Millennial reign governed from the same city. All three traditions converge on Jerusalem as the theatre of the end; all three diverge, sometimes sharply, on virtually every other question — who the Messiah is, what he will do, how history will conclude, and what lies beyond it.

The Jewish Tradition: A Human King of the Line of David

In Jewish theology, the Messiah (Mashiach, "the anointed one") is an entirely human figure — not divine, not pre-existent, not supernatural in his biological nature. He is a mortal king whose "miraculous" quality lies not in his person but in his achievements. The foundational requirement is non-negotiable in mainstream Jewish theology: the Messiah must be a direct patrilineal descendant of King David, and through David, of his father Jesse — as Isaiah writes, "there shall come forth a shoot from the stump of Jesse" (Isaiah 11:1).

The Jewish tradition does not include a concept of a "second coming." A figure who claims to be the Messiah but fails to accomplish the required tasks — the ingathering of the Jewish exiles to the Land of Israel, the rebuilding of the Temple, the bringing of universal peace, and the universal recognition of the one God — is simply not the Messiah; there is no provision for a partial first fulfilment followed by a later completion. This is the primary theological reason Judaism has historically rejected the messianic claims made on behalf of Jesus of Nazareth, and the same logic has applied to other messianic claimants throughout Jewish history, including Bar Kokhba in the second century and Sabbatai Zevi in the seventeenth. The Messianic Age itself, in the dominant strand of Jewish thought, is not the end of the world but the transformation of it: war ceases, hunger ends, hatred is overcome, and the knowledge of God fills the earth as the waters cover the sea (Isaiah 11:9) — an earthly era of unprecedented flourishing, not a supernatural dissolution of the created order.

Dispensational Christianity: The Divine-Human Christ

Dispensational Christianity begins from a starting point categorically different from either of the other two traditions: the belief that Jesus of Nazareth was and is both fully divine and fully human — the eternal Son of God incarnate in human flesh, the universal consensus of historic Christianity. What Dispensationalism adds is a specific eschatological architecture distinguishing sharply between God's purposes for Israel and his purposes for the Church, as two distinct peoples with two distinct destinies.

Dispensationalism was developed primarily by the Anglo-Irish theologian John Nelson Darby in the 1830s and 1840s, and popularised through the Scofield Reference Bible (first published 1909), which embedded Dispensational notes directly into the biblical text and shaped the reading of an entire generation of English-speaking evangelicals. In the twentieth century, Hal Lindsey's The Late Great Planet Earth (1970) and Tim LaHaye's Left Behind series brought Dispensational eschatology into mainstream popular culture.

The central organising principle is the division of human history into distinct "dispensations," in each of which God relates to humanity under a different covenant arrangement. The current dispensation — the "Church Age" — is understood as a parenthesis in God's primary programme for the nation of Israel, temporarily set aside when Israel rejected its Messiah at the first coming. At the end of the Church Age, the Church will be "raptured," and God's programme for Israel will resume, culminating in the Second Coming of Christ and the establishment of the Millennial Kingdom — a literal, physical, visible return of the same Jesus who ascended from the Mount of Olives, returning "in like manner" (Acts 1:11), now in glory rather than humility.

The Islamic Tradition: Isa, the Prophet and Messenger

In Islam, Jesus — Isa ibn Maryam (Jesus son of Mary) — occupies a position of the highest honour as one of the greatest prophets, born of a virgin by divine miracle, given the Gospel (Injil) as his scripture, and capable of extraordinary miracles by God's permission. He is not, however, divine — the Islamic position is unequivocal that there is no god but Allah, and to ascribe divinity to any created being, including Jesus, is shirk, the gravest of all sins.

The Quran addresses the crucifixion directly, stating in Surah An-Nisa (4:157-158) that Jesus was neither killed nor crucified — that what appeared to the people was a likeness — and that Allah raised him to himself. Islamic theology therefore holds that Jesus did not die on the cross, did not rise from the dead in the Christian sense, and is currently alive with God, awaiting his return at the appointed time. Upon his return, Isa is expected to descend — most narrations describe Damascus — and join forces with the Mahdi, the divinely guided leader already uniting the Muslim community. His primary mission is the defeat of the Dajjal (the Antichrist) and the restoration of true monotheistic worship. He will "break the cross" — a symbolic repudiation of the doctrine of Christ's crucifixion and divinity — and live as a Muslim, dying a natural death after approximately forty years.

The Quran also states, in Surah An-Nisa (4:159), that before his death, every person of the People of the Book — every Jew and every Christian — will believe in him, interpreted to mean his return will resolve the theological disputes between the Abrahamic faiths by making the truth of his prophetic status unmistakable.

A Fourth Voice, and a Shared Threshold

Running throughout the Islamic section of this study is the voice of Dr. Imran Hosein, whose geopolitical interpretation of Islamic eschatology has attracted significant attention, and who departs in some respects from the wider Islamic scholarly consensus. His perspective is presented as the study's primary Islamic eschatological framework, with points of divergence from other Islamic voices noted where they are significant.

Beyond the identity of the Messiah, the study traces in detail the single eschatological event that appears, in some form, in all three traditions: the conflict of Gog and Magog — described in the Hebrew Bible in Ezekiel 38-39, in the New Testament in Revelation 20:7-9, and in Islamic scripture and hadith as the release of Yajuj and Majuj. In each tradition, this event functions as a pivotal threshold: the final convulsion of opposition to divine sovereignty that precedes the establishment of the ultimate order. This study traces that conflict through each tradition before drawing systematic comparisons across timing, participants, theological function, and resolution — attending, throughout, to what each tradition actually says on its own terms, before asking what those different accounts share and where they part ways.
