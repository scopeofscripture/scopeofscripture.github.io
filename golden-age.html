The Golden Age — Three Traditions, One Final Chapter

Few questions have occupied the human imagination more persistently than the question of how history ends. Across cultures, centuries, and civilisations, the conviction that the present order of things is not final — that something decisive lies ahead, something that will resolve the tensions, injustices, and incompleteness of the present age — has proven remarkably resilient. It surfaces in mythology, in philosophy, and above all in the great monotheistic religious traditions that have shaped the moral and political landscape of the world for three millennia.

This study focuses on three of those traditions — Judaism, Christianity (specifically in its Dispensational expression), and Islam — and on a fourth voice that speaks from within the Islamic tradition while departing from it in significant respects: the contemporary Islamic scholar Dr. Imran Hosein. All three traditions are Abrahamic, tracing their origins to the covenant made between God and Abraham. All three regard Jerusalem as a city of unique sacred significance. And all three anticipate, in their own distinctive ways, a final chapter of human history in which divine sovereignty is fully and visibly established over a world that has, for most of its duration, resisted it.
