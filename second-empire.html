The Second Empire — The City of London and the Architecture of Offshore Wealth

As the formal British Empire declined through the second half of the twentieth century, the British establishment did not simply disappear along with the colonies, governors, and garrisons. Instead, it transformed. Rather than ruling through armies and colonial administrations, it built what can be described as a second empire: a hidden, global financial network, with the City of London — a distinct, semi-autonomous financial district with its own unique legal status — at its center.

This network's structure mirrors, in striking ways, the geography of the empire it replaced. Its central hub is the City of London itself, supported by a "spider's web" of former colonies and current overseas territories — the Cayman Islands, Jersey, Bermuda, and others — that provide the secrecy and light-touch regulation the network depends on. Britain maintains real control over many of these jurisdictions, appointing governors and senior officials, while relying on subtle, informal, closed-door arrangements to preserve the appearance that these territories operate independently.
