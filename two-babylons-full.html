The Two Babylons — Alexander Hislop's Landmark Work

First published as a pamphlet in 1853 and greatly expanded in 1858, The Two Babylons, or The Papal Worship Proved to Be the Worship of Nimrod and His Wife remains one of the most widely circulated religious texts of the nineteenth century. Written by Alexander Hislop (1807-1865), a theologian of the Presbyterian Free Church of Scotland, the book has sold millions of copies, been translated into numerous languages, and continues to influence Protestant and Reformed thought today.

Hislop lived during an era of intense religious ferment — the rise of biblical criticism, the expansion of the British Empire, and a renewed interest in the archaeology of the ancient Near East. As a minister in the Free Church of Scotland, a denomination born from a schism over state control of the church, he was steeped in a tradition that emphasised the purity of Scripture and viewed Roman Catholicism with clear-eyed suspicion. His book emerged from this milieu, combining theological conviction with an ambitious reconstruction of ancient history.

One Religion, Two Babylons

The Two Babylons demonstrates that the Roman Catholic Church is not a legitimate development of apostolic Christianity, but the modern continuation of the ancient pagan religion of Babylon. Hislop identifies the "Mystery Babylon the Great, the Mother of Harlots and Abominations of the Earth" of Revelation 17:5 not as a merely symbolic portrait of ancient idolatry, but as a prophetic identification of the Roman Catholic Church itself — the woman seated on seven mountains, drunk with the blood of the saints, arrayed in purple and scarlet, a precise description of the papal system.

Nimrod and the First Babylon

Hislop opens by identifying Nimrod, the "mighty hunter" of Genesis 10:8-9, as the founder both of the city of Babylon and of the world's first false religion. After Nimrod's death, his wife Semiramis preserved and expanded this system, declaring that Nimrod had become a god and presenting her son — variously called Tammuz, Horus, or other names in different cultures — as his reincarnation. Semiramis was worshipped as the "Queen of Heaven," the "Mother of God," and the source of divine grace; her son was venerated as a saviour figure. Together with the deified Nimrod, they formed a trinity that became the template for pagan religion across the globe — a mother goddess, a divine child, priestly celibacy, infant consecration, image veneration, and a host of shared rituals and festivals.

The Migration of Babylon

Hislop traces the "migration" of this Babylonian system across civilisations — appearing in Egypt as the worship of Isis and Horus; in Greece as Rhea and Zeus, or Ceres and Bacchus; in Rome as Venus and Jupiter, or Cybele and her divine child; in India as Isi and Iswara; in Scandinavia as Frigga and her son. He supports this with hundreds of citations from classical historians, ancient poets, and early church fathers, drawing out the common elements — mother goddesses, divine infants, sacred trees, circular worship spaces, priestly castes, secret initiations — as evidence of a single underlying system that spread from Babylon to the ends of the earth, never truly destroyed but simply disguised.

The Assimilation into Christianity

The central mechanism of Hislop's argument is that when Christianity became the state religion of the Roman Empire under Constantine, it did not so much convert the pagans as the pagans converted the outward forms of the church. The old Babylonian religion had already established itself in Rome through the worship of Cybele and other imported deities; when the Roman Church rose to dominance, it took over the existing infrastructure, titles, and practices of this pagan system while applying Christian names to them — retaining the substance of Babylonian worship while claiming the authority of Christ.

Key Parallels

The bulk of the book consists of detailed chapters comparing specific Catholic doctrines and practices to their pagan origins. On the Mass, Hislop traces the Catholic doctrine of transubstantiation to pagan mysteries in which worshippers ate the body of their god in the form of sacred cakes, and the round wafer to the ancient symbol of the sun. On Marian devotion, he presents the veneration of the Virgin Mary as a direct continuation of the worship of Semiramis and her counterparts — the titles "Queen of Heaven," "Mother of God," and "Mediatrix of All Graces" identical to those given pagan mother goddesses, and the image of Mary holding the infant Jesus mirroring Semiramis with Tammuz in Babylon, Isis with Horus in Egypt, and Cybele with her divine child in Rome.

On the Papacy, Hislop identifies the Pope as the modern successor to the pagan high priest of Rome, the Pontifex Maximus — the very title "Pontiff" a pagan one, and the triple crown said to derive from the mitre of the priests of Dagon, the fish-god. On Lent and Easter, he traces the forty-day fast to the ancient mourning of women for Tammuz described in Ezekiel 8:14, and Easter itself to the worship of Astarte or Ishtar, the Babylonian goddess of fertility whose symbol was the egg and whose sacred animal was the hare — the very name "Easter" preserving hers. On Christmas, he notes that December 25 was the birthday of the "unconquered sun" (Natalis Invicti) in Roman paganism, with the Christmas tree, yule log, and gift-giving all customs with pre-Christian roots.

Hislop extends the same method to infant baptism (linked to pagan initiation rites consecrating children to the gods), to religious orders and clerical celibacy (traced to the priesthoods of pagan mother-goddess cults), to purgatory and prayers for the dead (traced to ancient beliefs in an intermediate purifying fire), to the sign of the cross itself (questioning whether the Greek word stauros even denotes a cross rather than a simple stake, and noting the cross's pre-Christian use as a pagan solar emblem), and to the veneration of relics and images (paralleled with the pagan worship of heroes' bones and animated cult statues).

The Prophetic Dimension

In Hislop's reading, the fall of ancient Babylon was not the end of the Babylonian religion but merely its relocation — migrating to Pergamos, then to Rome, and finally into the Christian church, where it survives to this day under Christian disguise. The call of Revelation 18:4 — "Come out of her, my people, that ye be not partakers of her sins" — is presented as a direct command to every true believer to separate from the Roman Catholic Church and its practices.

A Landmark of Reformation Literature

The Two Babylons is a landmark in Reformation literature — a powerful witness of the biblical text, standing in the same tradition as the Protestant Reformers who correctly referenced Scripture even under the most draconian conditions, conditions that resulted in their own ghastly deaths for the sake of that testimony. Supplying credible evidence and rigorous methodology, dense with footnotes and citations from classical authors such as Herodotus, Ovid, and Virgil, from the Church Fathers, and from the archaeological discoveries of his day, it certainly meets the standards of serious scholarship. Hislop correctly demonstrates that the only true Christianity is that which adheres strictly to the Bible, free from the traditions, rituals, and hierarchical structures that are pagan imports — presenting the Reformation as an unfinished work, and appealing to readers to examine the evidence for themselves and reject any religious practice that cannot be found in Scripture alone.

For those seeking to understand the history of Christian-pagan interaction, The Two Babylons is best read as a reliable guide and as a historical artefact — a window into the truth of Rome's counterfeit Christianity.
